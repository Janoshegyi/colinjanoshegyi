Why Indonesian Football Will Never Produce a World Class Player

Structural economics, not talent. A look at how broken incentives, rapid naturalization policies, and institutional instability are failing 270 million people.

Two hundred and seventy million people. The fourth most populous country on Earth. A nation where football is not just a sport , it is a religion, a language, a reason to skip work on a Tuesday afternoon. And yet, in the entire history of professional football, Indonesia has not produced a single player who has competed at the highest level of the game. Not one.

Brazil has 215 million people and gave the world Pelé, Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, and Neymar. Argentina produced Messi and Di María in the same generation from 46 million. Croatia , four million people, a nation that emerged from war in the 1990s , has had Modrić, Šuker, Boban, and Rakitić. The easy answer to Indonesia's absence from this list is talent. That Indonesians simply aren't built for football. That answer is lazy. And it is wrong. The real answer is money , and what a broken system chooses to do with it.

What Football Actually Means Here

Before the economics, you need to understand the stakes. This is not a country that is indifferent to football. The Jakmania , Persija Jakarta's supporter group , has over four million registered members. That is more than the entire population of New Zealand. Aremania, Bobotoh, Bonek , these are not fan clubs. These are movements. Indonesian supporters have literally died for their clubs. Rivalries here are not metaphorical.

And then there is Timnas , the national team. Every time Indonesia qualifies for a regional tournament, the entire country stops. Restaurants empty. Traffic disappears. Seventy, eighty thousand people pack into Gelora Bung Karno, a stadium older than the nation itself. The hope is always enormous. The heartbreak, almost always guaranteed.

"Why does all of this love, all of this passion, all of this obsession , why does none of it produce greatness?"

The gap between passion and performance is not an accident. It is an outcome. And to understand it, you have to go back to where Indonesian football started , and who was allowed to control it.

A History of Dysfunction

PSSI , the Football Association of Indonesia , was founded in 1930, making it one of the oldest football associations in Asia. Indonesia even sent a team to the 1938 World Cup under Dutch colonial rule, before most of Europe had qualified. But independence brought a different kind of problem: football became a political tool. Presidents, military generals, and regional governors used club ownership and association control to build loyalty and signal prestige. The sport that should belong to the people began to belong to the powerful.

The clearest symptom came in 2011, when FIFA banned Indonesia from international competition , not for match-fixing or doping, but because the government directly attempted to take over PSSI and install their own appointees. FIFA's rules on government interference are unambiguous. Indonesia ignored them. The country that invented one of Asia's oldest football associations was suspended from the game it helped build.

The ban was lifted. But nothing structurally changed. Between 2003 and 2020, Indonesia cycled through over twelve different national team head coaches. Some lasted less than a year. One lasted three months. You cannot build a football culture on a foundation that resets every twelve months. By 2023, Indonesia still ranked below Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines in the Asian Football Confederation rankings , countries with a fraction of the population, a fraction of the passion, and in some cases, a fraction of the budget.

The Economics of Underachievement

The BRI Liga 1 , Indonesia's top professional football league , looks serious on paper. Twenty-two clubs. Millions of viewers. Broadcast deals with major platforms. It is the most watched football league in Southeast Asia by local viewership. But here is where the numbers become uncomfortable.

The total annual revenue of Liga 1, across all clubs combined, is estimated at roughly 300 to 400 billion rupiah , approximately 20 to 25 million US dollars. That sounds substantial until context is applied. Manchester City spends more than that in a single week on wages. The comparison is not chosen for shock value; it illustrates the structural gap with precision.

The Thai Premier League , a country with 70 million people, less than a third of Indonesia's population , generates nearly three times the Liga 1 figure. Japan's J-League generates twenty times more. Revenue alone does not determine player development. But it funds everything that does: academies, coaching staff, sports science, nutrition programmes, scouting networks, youth tournaments. These are not luxuries. They are the pipeline.

The Academy Problem

A credible football academy costs a minimum of 2 to 3 million dollars per year to operate properly , qualified coaches, facilities that meet international standards, full-time medical staff, and educational support for young players. The average club academy budget in Indonesia is estimated at under 500,000 dollars annually. Many operate with part-time coaches holding no UEFA or AFC-equivalent licenses.

Compare that to Gamba Osaka's academy in Japan, or the youth development programme at Jeonbuk Hyundai Motors in South Korea , clubs with full-time sports scientists, psychologists, and tactical analysts dedicated exclusively to under-12s. Indonesia's best academies are producing players for Liga 1. Japan's best academies are producing players for the Bundesliga. That is not a talent gap. That is an investment gap.

Comfortable Wages and the Incentive Trap

The average Liga 1 player earns between 30 and 80 million rupiah per month , roughly 2,000 to 5,000 US dollars. For Indonesia, that is an upper-middle-class income. It is comfortable. And comfortable is the enemy of greatness.

When a young Indonesian player with genuine potential reaches Liga 1, they face a choice that players in Europe rarely encounter. Leave , go abroad, earn less initially, navigate language barriers, compete against far better players, and risk public failure. Or stay in Indonesia, be treated like a local celebrity, earn a good salary, and never be truly tested. Without financial pressure or institutional infrastructure pushing players abroad, the rational economic decision is to stay.

"Individual rationality. Collective failure."

Thailand and Vietnam have addressed part of this problem deliberately. Their associations partnered with foreign clubs to create loan pipelines , young Thai players at clubs in Japan, Belgium, the Netherlands, with government subsidy behind them. Indonesia has no equivalent programme. No structural pathway. You either make it abroad alone, or you do not make it abroad at all.

The Croatia Verdict

Croatia is the most instructive comparison available , not because it is flattering, but because it removes every convenient excuse. In the 1990s, Croatia had a GDP per capita not dramatically different from Indonesia's. A nation emerging from war. Limited infrastructure. Four million people. And yet it has produced Luka Modrić, Davor Šuker, Zvonimir Boban, Ivan Rakitić, and a generation of players who have competed at the highest levels of club and international football.

What Croatia had was a deliberate system. The Croatian Football Federation created a centralised youth development model. Every qualified youth coach in the country was trained under the same methodology. Dinamo Zagreb's academy became a deliberate feeder to Europe. The culture was not 'play football' , it was 'become European-ready.'

Luka Modrić did not become Luka Modrić because Croatia is genetically gifted. He became Modrić because at nine years old, he entered a system that knew exactly what to do with him. Indonesia has found no equivalent , not because there isn't one. But because the nine-year-old version of him is probably playing on a concrete pitch with no coach, no pathway, and no structural reason to believe the system will catch him.

It Is Not Biology. It Is Choice.

Individual exceptions exist. Egy Maulana Vikri went to Lechia Gdańsk. Witan Sulaeman tried his luck in Europe. Marselino Ferdinan is the most technically gifted young player the country has produced in a generation. But individual exceptions do not make a system. And a system that relies on exceptions is not a development model , it is a lottery.

Every structural incentive in Indonesian football , the political control, the low academy investment, the comfortable domestic wages, the absence of a foreign pathway , points away from producing greatness. This is not an accident. It is the cumulative result of choices made at every level of the sport's governance, repeated over decades.

There are kids in Makassar, in Medan, in Papua , with the physical tools, the hunger, and the football intelligence to play at the highest level. The question has never been whether the talent exists. Brazil does not produce great players because Brazilians are born with something different in their blood. Brazil produces great players because Brazil built a machine that turns talent into greatness. Indonesia has the talent. It just has not decided to build the machine.
