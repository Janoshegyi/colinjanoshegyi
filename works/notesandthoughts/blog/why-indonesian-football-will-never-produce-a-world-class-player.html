Why Indonesian Football Will Never Produce a World Class Player

Structural economics, not talent. A look at how broken incentives, rapid naturalization policies, and institutional instability are failing 284 million people.

Indonesia has more than 284 million people, the fourth most populous country on Earth, and a nation where football is not just a sport but a religion, a language, a reason to stay up all night for the Champions League with work in the morning. And yet, in the entire history of professional football, Indonesia has not produced a single player who has competed at the highest level of the game. Not one.

A note on terms, because they matter here. By "world-class" I mean a regular at a top European club or in a World Cup-level national team. By "produced" I mean developed in Indonesia, a very different thing from holding an Indonesian passport from the process of naturalization. That difference has never mattered more, and the naturalization section shows why.

Brazil has 215 million people and gave the world Pelé, Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, and Neymar. Argentina produced Messi and Di María in the same generation from 46 million. Croatia, almost four million people, a nation that emerged from war in the 1990s, has had Modrić, Šuker, Boban, and Rakitić. The easy answer to Indonesia's absence from this list is talent. That Indonesians simply aren't built for football. That answer is lazy. And it is wrong. The real answer is money, and what a broken system chooses to do with it.

What Football Actually Means Here

Before the structure, let's understand the stakes. This is not a country indifferent to football. Gelora Bung Karno, the national stadium in Jakarta, opened in July 1962 for that year's Asian Games, seventeen years after independence. Since its 2018 renovation it has held 77,193 seats. A 1985 match between Persib Bandung and PSMS Medan reportedly drew 150,000. Supporters here are not casual. A home game for Timnas, the national team, stops the country.

The gap between passion and performance is not an accident. It is an outcome. And to understand it, you have to start with who has run the game.

A History of Dysfunction

PSSI, the Football Association of Indonesia, was founded in 1930. In 1938 a team representing the Dutch East Indies became the first Asian side to play at a World Cup, and lost 6-0 to Hungary in Reims. Indonesia later became a founding member of the Asian Football Confederation, but it has never been back to the finals. In 1986, South Korea knocked it out in the second round of Asian qualifying.

Off the pitch, instability has been the pattern. A feud between PSSI and a breakaway association once produced two rival leagues. In April 2015 the sports minister froze PSSI, and on 30 May 2015 FIFA suspended Indonesia for government interference. The domestic season was cancelled, clubs and national teams were barred from FIFA and AFC competitions, and Indonesian football lost access to AFC and FIFA development programmes while the ban lasted. FIFA lifted the suspension in May 2016.

The national team has seen the same churn. Shin Tae-yong was sacked in January 2025 while Indonesia was still chasing a place at the 2026 World Cup. Patrick Kluivert replaced him and was dismissed in October 2025. John Herdman was appointed on 3 January 2026. Three head coaches in just over a year. You cannot build a football culture on a foundation that resets every few months.

The recent past is not all bad, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Indonesia reached the Asian Cup round of 16 for the first time in January 2024, reached the final group stage of World Cup qualifying for the first time, and then went on to a fourth round. It still did not qualify, and in the July 2026 FIFA rankings it sat 118th, behind Vietnam (99th) and Thailand (94th).

What the Numbers Actually Show

To compare leagues I'm using Transfermarkt's estimates of total squad market value. They are estimates, and they measure what players are worth rather than what clubs earn — but they treat every league the same way, and anyone can check them.

At the start of the 2025-26 season, Transfermarkt valued Thailand's top flight at about €76.8 million and Indonesia's at about €71.5 million. Indonesia's league (now called the BRI Super League, with 18 clubs) sat ahead of Vietnam's V.League 1 at €53.7 million.

By regional standards, Indonesia's league is not poor. That rules out the convenient excuse. The real gap is between Southeast Asia and the leagues that produce elite players. At the start of 2025-26, Manchester City's squad alone was valued at about €1.32 billion, roughly 18 times the combined value of every squad in Indonesia's top flight.

Two things follow. No league in the region is big enough to turn a promising teenager into a world-class player on its own, so the route runs through Europe. And league wealth doesn't explain the differences inside the region: Indonesia's league is valued well above Vietnam's, yet Vietnam ranks 19 places higher.

The Pathway Problem

If a domestic league can't produce elite players alone, a young Indonesian needs a way to reach somewhere that can. People have tried. PSSI has sent young players to Italy and Uruguay through its Primavera and SAD programmes, and to England through Garuda Select, whose third edition picked 21 players in 2020 for training there. In 2018 the teenager Egy Maulana Vikri signed a three-year deal with Lechia Gdańsk in Poland's top flight and made his Ekstraklasa debut that December.

The Indonesian who has actually reached one of Europe's top leagues came through none of those routes. Jay Idzes was born in Mierlo in the Netherlands and came up through PSV's academy. He made his Serie A debut for Venezia in August 2024 and told the PSSI website he was "very proud to be the first Indonesian player to play in Serie A." He took Indonesian citizenship in December 2023 through his mother's family, joined Sassuolo in 2025, and captains the national team.

None of this is a criticism of him. It simply shows where the first Indonesian to play in Serie A was actually built, and it was not in Indonesia.

The Naturalization Shortcut

Under chairman Erick Thohir, the federation has leaned heavily on recruiting players abroad who qualify through Indonesian ancestry. Databoks counted 19 of the 30 players (63%) on the provisional list for the March 2025 World Cup qualifiers as naturalized, all of them with Indonesian parents or grandparents. For the June 2024 matchday squad, the share was 43%.

Those years also brought new highs for the team: a first Asian Cup knockout round in January 2024 and a first appearance in the final group stage of World Cup qualifying. There is nothing improper in the approach: Indonesia's version rests on ancestry, and a federation under pressure to win would be tempted by it anywhere.

But this answers a different question from the one this article asks. It makes the national team stronger. It does not show that Indonesia can develop elite players itself, and it removes the pressure to build the systems that would. This is the incentive trap at national scale: every naturalized call-up is a result the federation did not have to build a pipeline to earn, and a pipeline pays off in fifteen years while the next qualifier is next month. A national team full of players developed in Dutch academies says nothing about whether a child in Medan or Makassar has a realistic path.

The Croatia Verdict

Croatia is the most instructive comparison available, because it removes the population excuse. With 3.8 million people, it finished third at its first World Cup in 1998, after the break-up of Yugoslavia, and as of 2022 had missed only one World Cup since. It reached the 2018 final and finished third again in 2022.

The AP puts Croatia's success down to a passionate fan base and elite youth academies at clubs such as Dinamo Zagreb. FIFA's president has called the Croatian federation an example of an association punching above its weight, and FIFA Forward funding has supported its grassroots development. Luka Modrić, the 2018 world player of the year, was born in Zadar, a city of fewer than 100,000 people, and learned the game in its streets and car parks.

Luka Modrić did not become Luka Modrić because Croatia is genetically gifted. He became Modrić because, at nine years old, he entered a system that knew what to do with his talent.

Indonesia has likely produced children with similar potential. The difference is not ability, but structure. Somewhere, the Indonesian equivalent of a young Modrić is playing on a concrete pitch without coaching, without a development pathway, and without any real reason to believe the system will ever find him. Meanwhile, the same institutions that claim to invest in children struggle to provide even the most basic nutrients standard reliably.

What Is Still Missing

Exceptions exist. Egy Maulana Vikri went to Lechia Gdańsk. Witan Sulaeman tried his luck in Europe. Marselino Ferdinan is the most technically gifted young player the country has produced in a generation. But individual exceptions do not make a system. And a system that relies on exceptions is not a development model, it is a lottery.

Four problems run through everything above, and not one of them is talent. Governance has been unstable, from the 2015 suspension to the revolving door of national team coaches. The pathway to Europe is thin: overseas programmes have run, but I couldn't find one that produced an established starter at a top European club. The domestic league is small by global standards. And the latest strategy imports results instead of building the system that would produce them. Every one of these is a choice, repeated for decades.

So yes, "never" is the honest answer to the system as it stands today. It is not a verdict on the children.

There are kids in Makassar, in Medan, in Papua — with the physical tools, the hunger, and the football intelligence to play at the highest level. The question has never been whether the talent exists. Brazil does not produce great players because Brazilians are born with something different in their blood. Brazil produces great players because Brazil built a machine that turns talent into greatness. Indonesia has the talent. It just has not decided to build the machine.
