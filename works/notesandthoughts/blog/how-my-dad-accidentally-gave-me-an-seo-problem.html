How My Dad Accidentally Gave Me an SEO Problem

Competing against the abundance of Colin Johns on the internet.

When I first thought about building my personal website, I assumed ranking for my own name would be the easiest SEO task I could ever do. After all, how competitive could a personal name really be?

Then I searched for "Colin John."

That was when I realized my dad had unknowingly given me an SEO problem before I was even born.

I wasn't competing against a handful of people. I was competing against an abundance of Colin Johns. I was competing against a renowned musician from Memphis, a deputy commissioner from St. Vincent and the Grenadines, a cardiac surgeon from Bengaluru, and many other Colin Johns who had spent years building their own reputations online. Google had absolutely no reason to believe that I was the Colin John it should have shown. From an SEO perspective, I was practically invisible.

After spending several years studying in Hungary, I ended up taking a piece of the country with me. Although I would love to say that piece was my beloved Túró Rudi, which honestly deserves its own article, the thing I carried with me was a last name with a story of its own.

I started using a new family name: Jánoshegyi.

The funny thing is that Janoshegyi roughly translates to "John Hill" and is also the name of the highest point in Budapest. Ironically, despite my surname's connection to a hill, I've long imagined building a life in the Netherlands.

The name gave me something that John never could: uniqueness. It also gave me countless opportunities to watch people pause for a second and wonder how on earth Janoshegyi is supposed to be pronounced.

Suddenly, I was no longer competing with every Colin John on the planet. I had a name that was distinctly mine. Yet even then, Google still needed answers. Who is Colin Janoshegyi? What does he do? Why should this website be considered the primary source of information about him?

Those questions eventually led me down a path that I never originally intended to take. Instead of treating my website as a portfolio, I started treating myself as an SEO project.

Building My Own Entity

As I dug deeper, I realized Google wasn't really trying to understand my website. It was trying to understand me.

In SEO terms, I wasn't just building a website. I was building an entity profile.

Google is constantly trying to understand entities.

Companies are entities. Places are entities. Concepts are entities. People are entities too. My goal was no longer to rank pages, but to strengthen the signals that define an entity called Colin Janoshegyi.

Getting the website indexed wasn't the hard part. Anybody can put a sign on the highway. The real challenge was making sure people (and search engines) understood what that sign actually represented.

Every page, every article, every update was another piece of the story. A story that connected Colin Janoshegyi to SEO, marketing, digital strategy, and the University of Szeged. The website wasn't just being discovered. It was learning how to introduce itself.

I expanded my About section significantly. I removed content that was interesting to me but not particularly useful to visitors trying to understand who I was. Philosophy quotes and personal reflections gave way to information about my education, experience, skills, and professional interests.

For the first time, someone landing on my website could quickly answer a simple question: "Who is Colin Janoshegyi?"

The next step was making sure I existed outside of my own website.

After all, anyone can create a website and make claims about themselves. Search engines trust consistency. So I added images for visual entity signals. External profiles added off-page consistency signals. Internal linking created topical clustering.

Gradually, the picture became clearer. Google no longer saw an isolated website. It saw a person.

What Actually Worked

I wish I could tell you that I tracked everything from day one. That I had spreadsheets, ranking reports, and beautiful graphs showing my journey from page ten to page one.

I didn't. The truth is much less exciting. One afternoon, I typed "Colin John SEO" into Google out of curiosity. I wasn't expecting much. When my website appeared at the top, I actually refreshed the page because I thought I had personalized results turned on. Then I opened an incognito window and checked again.

Over time, searches such as "Colin John SEO," "Colin John Marketing," "Colin Janoshegyi SEO," and "Colin John University of Szeged" started returning my website as the strongest result.

The musician was still there. The deputy commissioner was still there. The cardiac surgeon was still there. But for the first time, I was there too.

What actually moved the needle wasn't a single tactic. It was the accumulation of consistent signals across multiple SEO layers.

At the technical level, there was indexation and crawl frequency, making sure Google could access everything efficiently.

At the content level, there was keyword alignment, not stuffing keywords, but consistently associating my name with terms like SEO, marketing, and digital strategy.

At the structural level, there was an internal linking architecture, which helped distribute authority across pages instead of isolating them.

And at the off-site level, there was entity consistency across the web: LinkedIn, Medium, SZTE University profile, and other mentions, reinforcing the same identity.

Over time, this created what SEO professionals often call entity consolidation: Google stops treating you as multiple disconnected mentions and starts treating you as one coherent identity.

Ranking is not always about keywords. Sometimes it is about identity. Sometimes the challenge is not convincing Google that your content is relevant, but convincing Google that you exist.

Lessons From Ranking My Own Name

The biggest lesson I learned is that personal branding and SEO are becoming increasingly difficult to separate.

A personal website is not just a digital business card. It is your source of truth.

Your About page matters more than most people think. Consistency across platforms matters. Images matter. Structured data matters.

Most importantly, writing about the things you want to be known for matters.

Google cannot associate your name with SEO if you never write about SEO. It cannot associate you with marketing if marketing never appears in your content.

Looking back, I think the experiment taught me something about how search itself has changed. Years ago, SEO often felt like a game of matching keywords to queries. Today, search engines are increasingly trying to understand concepts, relationships, and entities. In my case, the challenge was not convincing Google that my website contained the words "Colin John." The challenge was convincing Google that a real person named Colin Janoshegyi existed and that my website was the best source of information about him.

That distinction may seem subtle, but it changes how you approach SEO. Instead of focusing on individual keywords, I found myself focusing on identity, consistency, and context. The goal was no longer to optimize a page. It was to make it easier for search engines to understand who I was.

Competing against a cardiac surgeon was particularly humbling. If someone Googles "Colin John" because they need heart surgery, I hope Google keeps ranking him above me.

Today I rank for searches like "Colin John SEO" and "Colin Janoshegyi Marketing."

Tomorrow, perhaps I will finally rank for "Colin John."

And if that happens, I suppose I owe my dad a thank you for creating the challenge in the first place.
